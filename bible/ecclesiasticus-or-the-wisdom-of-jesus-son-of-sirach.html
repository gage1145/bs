Ecclesiasticus, or the Wisdom of Jesus, Son of Sirach

Name

In modern tradition, the book goes by three names that reflect three of its major language traditions. In most Greek manuscripts, it bears the title, “The Wisdom of Jesus, Son of Sirach.” Latin manuscripts generally have “The Book of Jesus, Son of Sirach,” but it is known in the Latin tradition as Ecclesiasticus, “Belonging to the Church,” a title that reflects its place in the Christian scriptural canon. No title proper survives in the ancient Hebrew manuscripts, but one medieval manuscript from the Cairo Genizah preserves an apparent title: “The Wisdom of Yeshua son of Eleazar son of Sira.” The rabbis refer to it as “The Book (or Instruction) of Ben Sira.” In modern use the shortened “Sirach” can refer to the Greek tradition or to the entire book and “Ben Sira” to the sage himself, to the Hebrew tradition, or to the entire book.

Canonical Status

Sirach was never included in the Jewish canon. Yet some ancient Jews accorded it significance, as the fragmentary manuscripts found among the Dead Sea Scrolls and at Masada show. Even later rabbinic authorities continued to cite it. The Greek translation made by Ben Sira's grandson became part of the larger collection of translations called the Septuagint (LXX), which comprised the scriptures of the early Christian Church. Roman Catholics and Orthodox Christians, whose traditions have followed the Septuagint, have Sirach in their Old Testament among the wisdom books. The usual order is Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Solomon, Wisdom of Solomon, and Sirach/Ecclesiasticus. Sirach is not in the Protestant canon. During the Protestant Reformation, Martin Luther thought that the books of Christian scripture should match those of the Jewish Bible. He relegated Sirach (along with other “deuterocanonical” books) to the so-called Apocrypha, and in his German translation he placed them between the Old and New Testaments, where they are located in many modern Protestant editions of the Bible.

Author, Date, and Historical Context

Sirach is one of only a few early Jewish texts in which the author identifies himself. In 50.27 he gives his name as “Yeshua (Joshua/Jesus) son of Eleazar son of Sira.” The Greek translation of the verse identifies him as a Jerusalemite. He further indicates that he operated a “house of instruction” (Heb bet midrash, 51.23) where he trained young men for careers as scribes and sages, likely to work in the official bureaucracy as he did.

Ben Sira wrote in Hebrew ca. 180 bce, a date that can be deduced from several pieces of evidence. In ch 50 Ben Sira refers to the high priest Simon II (219–196 bce) in language suggesting that Simon was already dead. Moreover, Ben Sira shows no awareness of the conflicts with the Seleucid monarch Antiochus IV Epiphanes after 175 bce that resulted in the Maccabean revolt. Finally, in the Prologue to the Greek translation, the grandson says that he came to Egypt “in the thirty-eighth year of the reign of Euergetes the king” (Ptolemy VIII Euergetes II), ca. 132 bce. If one moves backward from grandson to grandfather, Ben Sira's date is ca. 180 bce as well.

Ben Sira's time was one of political turmoil. Around 200 bce, he experienced the transfer of control of ancient Judea from the Ptolemaic Empire in Egypt to that of the Seleucids in Syria. After a period of relative calm, the Romans defeated the Seleucid ruler Antiochus III and placed a heavy financial indemnity on him that his son Seleucus IV inherited. This situation likely resulted in economic hardship in Judea as Seleucus tried to raise funds for repayment. As a literate scribe and sage, Ben Sira would likely have worked for the priestly aristocracy that ruled Judea in this period, and he holds a very high view of the scribe/sage within Judean society (38.34c–39.11). He expresses strong support for Temple rituals and the Jerusalem priesthood (7.29–31; 35.1–13), and he portrays Simon II as an ideal high priest. Otherwise, specific references to contemporary events are difficult to identify, although his comments on kings and rulers may point to his attitude toward foreign domination of Judea. A scribe of Ben Sira's caliber might also travel extensively outside of Judea, perhaps in an official capacity (34.9–13).

Like all ancient literature, the Wisdom of Ben Sira is a product of its time. It reflects the social presumptions and mores of Ben Sira's world, the early second century bce. His views on women, children, and slaves, especially, reflect that time period—views that modern readers will not generally share. Ben Sira lived in a society that was governed by the values of honor and shame. A man's material wealth, along with his ability to control other culturally defined elements, such as his women, his political influence, and his reputation, determined his social standing. Maintaining that honor required constant vigilance. So while women, children, and slaves had little or no opportunity to gain honor for themselves, they had a significant impact on a man's honor. Ben Sira's teaching must be understood within his social context.

Literary History

Sirach has had a complicated textual history. Its main language of transmission has been the Greek translation—made by the author's grandson according to the Prologue—which was included in the Christian Old Testament. It serves as the basis for the English translation here, although with some supplementation from the Hebrew, Syriac, and Latin versions (see the textual notes). The Hebrew text fell into obscurity, although the rabbis knew some proverbs associated with the name of Ben Sira. Six fragmentary medieval Hebrew manuscripts of Sirach were discovered in the Genizah (storeroom) in a synagogue in Cairo at the end of the nineteenth century. Another fragmentary ancient manuscript came to light at Masada just west of the Dead Sea, and some fragments were found in Caves 2 and 11 among the Dead Sea Scrolls. Today about 70 percent of the book survives in Hebrew. Important translations were made in antiquity into Syriac and Latin. The text of Sirach underwent expansion in which proverbs were added to the book. These are given in the text in small type in the footnotes, which explains why some verse numbers are missing in the main text (see, for example, 1.4–6). In the Greek tradition all manuscripts contain a transposition where 30.25–33.13a and 33.13b–36.16a have been reversed, which accounts for the different chapter/verse numbering in some translations and scholarly studies. The present translation restores the text to its proper order.

Structure and Contents

Like the book of Proverbs, Sirach is an anthology or collection of smaller, individual units on a variety of subjects. The book has no discernible structure on which scholars have agreed. The self-praise of Wisdom, which is personified as a woman in ch 24, forms the centerpiece of the book, with other poems on seeking and finding wisdom punctuating the text (1.1–10; 14.20–15.10; 38.24–39.11). The book culminates in the praise of God's glory in creation (42.15–43.33) and the recital of Israel's “famous men” (44.1–49.16), which reaches its climax with the sage's glorious description of Simon II in language that evokes Ben Sira's picture of Wisdom herself (ch 50). Finally, the sage describes his own search for Wisdom in ch 51, which functions like an epilogue.

Ben Sira formulated his teaching using a wide array of literary forms, such as maxims or proverbs, analogies or comparisons (Heb meshalim), beatitudes and woes, numerical sayings, questions and answers, refrains and repetitions, prohibitions and admonitions, numerical sayings, hymns, aretalogy (self-praise), first-person accounts, and paraphrases of earlier texts. These techniques helped students (and later readers) retain his teaching, through which he shaped their understanding of their world and their ability to succeed in it. The Greek and Hebrew manuscript traditions include some titles that indicate new sections of the text. See, for example, in the NRSV titles before 23.7, 24.1, 30.1, 30.18, 44.1, 51.1, all of which are in Greek manuscripts. Hebrew Ms B from the Cairo Geniza preserves titles before 31.12 and 44.1.

Interpretation

Sirach is the longest wisdom book that we have from ancient Judaism, and it offers important insight into life in ancient Judea during a period for which we lack many other sources. While Ben Sira incorporates the insights of traditional ancient Near Eastern wisdom teaching on topics like friendship, speech, women, death, business, wealth, creation, and fear of the Lord, he innovates by bringing that traditional teaching under the umbrella of the distinctive traditions of Israel, particularly “the law that Moses commanded us” (24.23).

Ben Sira trained young men for careers as scribes and sages for public service, most likely in the bureaucratic employ of the priestly class. He strongly supported the Temple and the priests. Thus, he did not occupy the highest place on the social ladder, and his teaching reveals this in-between status. While he encouraged his trainees to seek the full measure of wisdom, he cautioned them about the many potential pitfalls that lay in their path. For example, he warns his charges to be wary when dealing with the rich, because one misstep could be disastrous (8.1–2). His notoriously negative attitude toward women, which makes his teaching difficult for contemporary readers, also stems from a framework of honor and shame, in which women's behavior can affect his students’ careers. Ben Sira struggles to maintain the traditional understanding of theodicy, in which the wise and righteous prosper and the foolish and wicked suffer. But his realization that the prosperous are often unwise or unrighteous and that the poor can outshine the rich in virtue tempers his views. With his doctrine of the “pairs” (33.7–15), Ben Sira attempts to account for evil, in the end ascribing all things to God. He does not seek to change the status quo with respect to the poor, and he is not an agent of social change. Although he exhorts his students to alleviate the suffering of the poor and oppressed “for the commandment's sake” (29.8–10), Ben Sira holds a traditional Israelite view of immortality that depends on one's children, one's good name, and the memory that one leaves behind (30.4–6; 38.16–23; 41.1–13).

THE PROLOGUE

Many great teachings have been given to us through the Law and the Prophets and the othersa that followed them, and for these we should praise Israel for instruction and wisdom. Now, those who read the scriptures must not only themselves understand them, but must also as lovers of learning be able through the spoken and written word to help the outsiders. So my grandfather Jesus, who had devoted himself especially to the reading of the Law and the Prophets and the other books of our ancestors, and had acquired considerable proficiency in them, was himself also led to write something pertaining to instruction and wisdom, so that by becoming familiar also with his bookb those who love learning might make even greater progress in living according to the law.

You are invited therefore to read it with goodwill and attention, and to be indulgent in cases where, despite our diligent labor in translating, we may seem to have rendered some phrases imperfectly. For what was originally expressed in Hebrew does not have exactly the same sense when translated into another language. Not only this book, but even the Law itself, the Prophecies, and the rest of the books differ not a little when read in the original.

When I came to Egypt in the thirty-eighth year of the reign of Euergetes and stayed for some time, I found opportunity for no little instruction.c It seemed highly necessary that I should myself devote some diligence and labor to the translation of this book. During that time I have applied my skill day and night to complete and publish the book for those living abroad who wished to gain learning and are disposed to live according to the law.

Prologue: Ben Sira's grandson composed a Prologue to the Greek translation that accompanies modern translations, since they are, for the most part, based on the Greek. In three elegant sentences (coinciding with the paragraphs in this translation), he tells about Ben Sira and his reason for writing, his own motivation for translating, and the difficulties of translating from Hebrew into Greek. He divides the literary heritage of Israel into three categories, two specific and one vague: the Law and the Prophets and the other books. Jesus is the Gk form of Heb “Yeshua,” which appears in 50.27 in a Hebrew manuscript (MS B). Instruction and wisdom could refer to the combination of Torah and proverbial wisdom characteristic of Ben Sira's book. The thirty-eighth year of the reign of Euergetes, 132 bce. Most scholars think that Ben Sira's grandson finished his translation somewhere shortly after Euergetes's death in 117 bce. The translation was for the Jewish community living abroad in Egypt so that they could benefit from his grandfather's wisdom. Note how he parallels language about his grandfather's intent with his own: love learning/gain learning; live according to the law.
1.1–10: Praise of Wisdom. God created Wisdom (Gk “sophia,” a feminine noun), which is probably meant to be a feminine figure, and gave her as a gift to all creation. 2–6: The rhetorical questions assume a negative answer, i.e., no human can know creation's secrets (cf. Job 38–39). 4: Created before all things, cf. 24.1–7; Prov 8.22–26; Wis 7.24–26. 9–10: Wisdom is a double gift, given to all his works, as well as specifically to those who love him (cf. Job 28.23–28).
1.11–30: Fear of the Lord is wisdom. Ben Sira establishes his three main theological themes in this poem: wisdom, fear of the Lord, and keeping the commandments, which are found in the Torah (24.23). 11: Fear of the Lord, the proper response to God's gift is awe and proper conduct (cf. Prov 1.7). 12: God rewards those who fear the Lord with long life, desirable goods (v. 17), and perfect health (v. 18); cf. v. 20; Prov 3.16; 22.4. 13: Happy end, immortality for Ben Sira consists primarily in leaving behind righteous children, along with a good name and reputation (40.19; 41.11–13). 14: Beginning of wisdom, Prov 1.7; 9.10; Ps 111.10. In the womb, a prophetic motif (49.7; Jer 1.5; Isa 44.2,24; 49.1,5; cf. Ps 139.13; Gal 1.15). 16: Wisdom is apportioned to all humans (cf. v. 19). 19: Heightened the glory of those who held her fast, see 24.19. 20: Wisdom is often pictured with imagery of plants, especially as a tree of life; see 24.13–17 (cf. Gen 3.22). 30: Before the whole congregation emphasizes Ben Sira's concern for honor and shame.
2.1–18: Trusting in God. How to remain faithful to God. 1: My child, lit., “my son,” a common way to refer to students in instructional literature (e.g., 3.17; 4.1; Prov 1.8; 2.1). 4–6: One must persevere in time of testing (v. 1), which is akin to refining gold (Prov 17.3; 27.21; Wis 3.6). 7–9: The threefold repetition of fear of the Lord (see 1.11n.) emphasizes wait, trust, and hope; see also vv. 15–17. 10: An appeal to the past to prove that trusting God brings reward (cf. Job 4.7; 8.8–10). 11: The Lord is compassionate and merciful, Ex 34.6–7; Ps 103.8; Jon 4.2. 12–14: The woe is a prophetic form, here used as a warning. The Lord's reckoning refers to recompense in this life. 15–16: His words and his ways are synonyms for his law. 18: Fall into the hands of the Lord, 2 Sam 24.14. Name, a reference to God as “Merciful One” (50.19).
3.1–16: Duties to parents. 1–2: God made the family hierarchical with parents above children. For the father as family head, see 7.23–26. On respect for both parents, see, e.g., Prov 1.8–9; 6.20; 20.20; 23.22; Ex 20.12; 21.15,17. 3–7: Honoring parents atones for sins (vv. 3,14; as do almsgiving and sacrifice; see 3.30; cf. 28.1–5) and brings long life (Ex 20.12; Deut 5.16). It thus equals fear of the Lord (1.12). 8–11: For the significance of a father's blessing, see Gen 27.1–29; 48.15–20; 49.1–28. Dishonoring parents brings disgrace, that is, shame. 16: The invoking of a blasphemer emphasizes that true piety is keeping God's commandments.
3.17–29: Humility. 17–20: Humility is a marker of piety (1.27) and so wins God's favor. 21: What is too difficult may refer to Jewish apocalyptic or Greek philosophical ideas about the secrets of creation; cf. Prov 30.18. 22: Wisdom and knowledge come from what is commanded (the Torah) and the proverbs (v. 29) of the sages, two foundational sources of wisdom for Ben Sira.
3.30–4.10: Almsgiving. 30–31: Atones for sin, cf. 3.3–4,14. 4.1–5: Ben Sira belongs to a class that has resources to help the needy, which the law commands (Deut 15.7–11). 6: Curse, cf. Ex 22.22–24; Deut 15.9. 10: On care for the widow and the orphan, see, e.g., Ex 22.22.
4.11–19: Submission to Wisdom. Wisdom's benefits are acquired through discipline. As in Prov 1.8–33; 8; Sir 24, Wisdom is personified as a woman. 14: Minister alludes to priestly service (cf. 24.10 regarding Wisdom). Holy One, a frequent title for God in Sirach (23.9; 43.10; 47.8; 48.20). 16: Faithful to Wisdom's teaching. 17–19: Wisdom's trials test the student's steadfastness. The term ordinances brings Torah and Wisdom together, anticipating Sir 24. 18: Reveal, acquiring wisdom has a revelatory aspect; cf. 39.7. 19: Cf. v. 16.
4.20–31: Shame and speech. An extended sequence of negative admonitions (do not) is a common stylistic feature in wisdom literature (see also 1.28–30; 4.1–6; 5; 7.1–26; cf. Prov 3.27–32). 20–21: For specifics on improper and proper shame, see 41.17–42.8. 23–26: Appropriate speech is a frequent topic in Sirach as well as in Jewish wisdom literature; cf. 19.6–17; 20.16–20; 22.27–23.15; 28.12–26; Prov 10.19; 12.6; 17.4. 26: Stop the current of a river, a metaphor for the impossibility of hiding sins from God. 28: Showing partiality to a ruler might require compromising one's integrity; see 7.6. 30: Lion, a frequent image in Sirach for ferocity (21.2; 25.16; 27.10,28; 28.23).
5.1–8: Avoid presumption. Do not presume that God will overlook sins. 1: Ben Sira is preoccupied with wealth, its acquisition, and use (5.8; 10.30; 14.3; 28.10; 31.1; 40.13). 4–6: See the liturgical formula in Ex 34.6–7 (and its later adaptations, e.g., Num 14.18; Pss 86.15; 103.8; 145.8) where God is slow to anger and shows mercy. Here, though, rather than as part of a promise, Ben Sira warns that God also brings wrath on the guilty. Cf. Sir 16.11–14. 7–8: For the sudden onset of calamity as punishment, see Prov 6.15; 24.22.
5.9–6.4: Speech, shame, and passion. 10: See 4.23–26n. 12: Hand over your mouth, a gesture of silence, humility, and submission (Job 29.9; 40.4; Prov 30.32). 14: Double-tongued, duplicitous or slandering (6.1; 28.13). 6.1: A bad name incurs shame shows the importance of reputation for Ben Sira (15.1–10; 37.26; 39.9,11; 40.19; 41.11–13; 44.1–15). 6.2–4: The transparent sexual image, withered tree, is the consequence of the grip of passion.
6.5–17: Friendship. One must exercise caution when choosing friends, since not all are of equal value (cf. 9.10; 12.8–9; 19.13–17; 22.19–26; 27.16–21; 37.1–6). Both wisdom literature (see, e.g., Prov 17.17; 18.24; 27.6,10) and Hellenistic writers (e.g., the gnomic poet Theognis, 575; 643–44; 697–98) deal with the topic. 15: Balance their worth, a reference to scales used to determine value.
6.18–37: Discipline and Wisdom. Acquisition of Wisdom means submitting to her harsh discipline, but the rewards are transformative. 18: My child, each section (see vv. 23,32) begins with this address. See 2.1n. When you have gray hair, the search for wisdom is a lifelong process. 19: Eat of her produce, cf. 24.19–22. 21: Like a heavy stone contrasts with toil but little (v. 19). 22: Wisdom is like her name, a play on Heb “musar” (“discipline”) and possibly “sur” (“turn away”). 23–31: Wisdom's discipline, conveyed with images of fetters and bonds, is transformed for the one who submits to it. 26: All your soul and all your might, see Deut 6.5. 30: Purple cord, a reminder of God's commandments (Num 15.38–40). 31: Collar … yoke, for the heavy labor of farm animals; cf. Mt 11.28–30. Glorious robe and splendid crown evoke priestly garments; see 45.7,12; 50.11. 32–37: The student should seek out and listen to the company of elders and meditate (see Ps 1.2) on God's commandments, two primary sources of wisdom for Ben Sira.
7.1–17: Miscellaneous advice. Negative admonitions (“Do not …”), see 4.20–31n. 4–7: Although a wise person might sit in the seat of honor or serve as a judge, ambition is tempered by the potential for disgrace. 9: See 5.6–7. Offering refers to a sacrifice. 15: Farm work, perhaps an allusion to Gen 2.15. For a different view of work and the sage, see 39.24–34. 17: Fire and worms, the Hebrew has only worms. Cf. Isa 66.24.
7.18–36: Social relations. Ben Sira's students are or will be heads of households who must manage a range of social relationships and responsibilities. 18: Ophir, southern Arabia, was famous for gold (1 Kings 9.28; 10.11; Job 22.24; 28.16; Ps 45.9; Isa 13.12). 19: Dismiss a … wife (see v. 26), Deut 24.1–4. 23: Discipline, or “instruct”; see 6.22n. 21: Freedom, see Ex 21.2; Lev 25.39–46; Deut 15.12. 24–25: Daughters, cf. 22.3–6n.; 42.9–13. 27–28: Father … mother, see 3.1–16n. 29–31: Wisdom literature rarely mentions priests and ritual obligations, but they are an important concern in Sirach (35.1–13; 45.6–25; 50.1–21). 29–30: With all your soul … with all your might, Deut 6.5. 31: Portion, Ex 29.27; Lev 7.31–34; Num 18.8–20; Deut 18.3–4. 32: Poor, Deut 15.7–11. 33: Dead, cf. 38.16–23. 36: The end of your life, see 1.13n.
8.1–19: Circumstances to avoid. A series of negative admonitions, as in 4.20–31; 7.1–21; 9.1–9, that highlight Ben Sira's ethics of caution. 1–2: Advice about the powerful and the rich reflects Ben Sira's precarious socioeconomic position. As a scribe, he serves the wealthy classes who thus have power over him. Gold … has perverted the minds of kings, cf. 10.8; Deut 17.14–20. 4: Your ancestors may be insulted for having a senseless descendant. 8–9: The sage serves in the employ of princes and other nobles. Thus the necessity of learning from the sages and the aged, who know the tradition and have experience. 11: Bring you to your feet, an image for an emotional reaction, where one might not guard speech and fall prey to the ambush of the insolent. 12–13: Surety, to offer property as collateral to secure the loan of another. See Prov 6.1–5; 11.15; 17.18; 22.26–27; 4QInstruction. Ben Sira does not forbid giving surety, but he advises extra caution (29.1–7,14–20). 18: The Hebrew plays on the words stranger (“zar”) and secret (“raz”).
9.1–9: Relations with women. More cautionary advice, here about women who might bring shame on the sage. 3: Loose, lit., “strange” or “foreign” (Heb “zarah”); see Prov 5.1–6; 7.4–27. 4: Singing girl, perhaps a prostitute; cf. Isa 23.15–16. 5: Penalties, on seducing a virgin, see Ex 22.16–17. 9: The penalty for adultery with another man's wife was death (Lev 20.10; Deut 22.22).
9.10–16: Making associations. 11–12: In Ben Sira's worldview, sinners might appear to have success, but God will hold them accountable. 13: Probably kings and rulers are meant. Battlements, high on the city walls where one is vulnerable to attack. 15–16: Proper associations should focus on the law of the Most High and fear of the Lord.
9.17–10.5: Wise rulers. A common topic in wisdom literature (Prov 25.4–5; 28.3,16; 29.4,12,14). 10.4–5: Cf. Dan 2.21.
10.6–18: Avoid arrogance and pride. 6–11: Arrogance is foolish in the face of human mortality. 9–11: Ben Sira's emphasis on rulers and nations may reflect the political conditions of his time, particularly the competition between the Ptolemaic and the Seleucid kings (see also vv. 14–17). 9: Dust and ashes, Gen 18.27; Job 30.19; 42.6. 12–18: Pride and sin go hand in hand. 14–15: The verbs overthrows, plucks up, destroys, and plants are reminiscent of Jer 1.10; see also Lk 1.52. 17: Removes … destroys; cf. Job 12.23; erases the memory of them, cf. Eccl 1.11.
10.19–11.6: Honor and humility. True honor accrues to those who fear the Lord (cf. Prov 1.7n) and keep the commandments. 19: Human offspring, the riddle gives the same answer to opposite questions. 24: Fear of the Lord trumps social status. 26–31: Although Ben Sira does not condemn wealth, the correlation of humility and honor reduces the social disparity between wealth and poverty. 11.3: For other comparisons between animal and human behaviors, see Prov 6.6–8; 30.15–19,24–31.
11.7–28: Deliberation and true wealth. Material security does not necessarily signal God's favor. 7–9: See 5.11–12. 14: Cf. Isa 45.6–7; Job 2.10. 17: His favor brings lasting success introduces a moral tension, since sinners often prosper; see v. 21. 21: The devout must trust that God will set things right. 28: One's deeds are reflected in the manner of one's death.
11.29–12.18: Caution in associations. These admonitions exemplify an ethic of caution. 33: Ruin your reputation reflects Ben Sira's concern for a good name (6.1; 15.6; 37.26; 39.10–11; 40.19; 41.11–13; 44.3,8,14). 12.1–7: Give alms to the devout only, because the ungodly will use them against the giver and God hates sinners. 11: One who polishes, that is, in order to be able to see the enemy clearly. Ancient mirrors were highly polished metal. 18: Shake … clap, gestures of derision.
13.1–24: Association with the rich. The sage must associate with the rich, and Ben Sira uses their treatment of the poor as a warning to his students about potential dangers. 2: Clay pot … iron kettle illustrate the difference in social status and the vulnerability of the sage; see vv. 15–20. 7: See 12.18. 8–9: See 31.12–18; Prov 23.1–3; Instruction of Amenemope 23. 13: Caution is the watchword, since any misstep can result in one's downfall. 15–20: Both the sinner and the rich are like predators, the devout and poor like prey, but see v. 24. 21–23: The rich often receive preferential treatment. Cf. Prov 14.20; 19.4.
13.25–14.2: Happiness. 26: A cheerful face reflects the inner condition of the heart, the seat of emotion and thought in ancient understanding.
14.3–19: Use of wealth. 3–10: The miser has the wrong approach to wealth. He is mean to himself and thus cannot see to help others (cf. Prov 11.24–26). 11–19: A contrast with the miser. The proper attitude is to enjoy your share of desired goods but also to present worthy offerings and do good to friends. 12: Hades, in Hebrew Sheol, the abode of the dead. 14: Eccl 8.15; 9.7. 17: Like a garment, Isa 50.9; Ps 102.26. You must die, Gen 2.17; 3.3. 18: Cf. Eccl 1.4; Iliad 6.146–49.
14.20–15.10. The benefits of wisdom. Whoever pursues Wisdom will receive benefits from her. 20: Happy is, a beatitude or macarism (cf., e.g., Ps 1.1; Prov 3.13; Mt 5.3–11; Lk 6.20–23; 4QBeatitudes). 22–25: Cf. Prov 8.32–35. The metaphors of hunter, lover (cf. Song 2.9), and neighbor emphasize single-minded pursuit. 26: On Wisdom as a tree, see 1.20n.; 24.13–17; Prov 3.18. 15.1: On wisdom, law, fears the Lord see 1.11–30n.; 6.32–37; 24.23. 2–3: Wisdom nurtures as a mother and loves as a young bride (cf. Prov 7.4; Wis 7.12; 8.2). Bread and water, cf. 1.16; 24.19–21; Prov 9.1–5. 6: Gladness and a crown of rejoicing, 1.11; 6.31. Everlasting name, immortality through a good reputation.
15.11–16.23: Free will and God's justice. God created humans with the capacity to choose, and so they are responsible for their sins, both individually and corporately. 11–13: Even though God created all things (see 11.14), God cannot do what he hates. 14–17: Humans have had free choice since the beginning, a likely interpretation of Gen 3. 14: Free choice (Heb “yetser”) can have both positive (Isa 26.3; 1 Chr 29.18) and negative (Gen 6.5; 8.1) connotations in the Hebrew Bible. In later Jewish texts, the idea develops that every person possesses a good and an evil “yetser.” 17: Life and death, Deut 30.15. 16.1–4: Having many children was seen as a good (Ps 127.3–5), and one's progeny could protect one's name and reputation (30.1–6). Children also can bring shame (cf. 22.3; 30.12–13). Thus, to die childless is preferable to having a thousand who are ungodly. 7–11: Since God punished the ancient giants (Gen 6.1–4; 1 En. 7; 10), the neighbors of Lot (Gen 18.16–19.29), the doomed nation of the Canaanites (Lev 18.3,25), and the six hundred thousand foot soldiers in the desert (Ex 12.37; Num 14.38), why would God overlook any single stiff-necked person? 12: According to his or her deeds, Ezek 18; Ps 62.12. 17–23: The fictitious speaker, a senseless and misguided person, argues that individuals are hidden from God in the vastness of the cosmos (cf. Job 22.13; Ezek 8.12). The unspoken answer is that God sees everything (23.19–20; 39.20) and judges.
16.24–17.24: Creation and repentance. A meditation on creation that moves from creation to the giving of the law to Israel, and individuals and repentance. 16.26–17.7: An interpretation of Gen 1–3. 26: From the beginning, Gen 1.1; Prov 8.22–23. Boundaries, Job 38.8–11; Pss 104.5–9; 148.5–6. 27–28: Creation is orderly and obeys God (see 33.14–15; 42.15–43.33), but contrast 1 En. 21. 29: Good things, Gen 1.4,10,12, etc. 30: Return, Gen 3.19; Ps 104.29. 17.1: Out of earth, Gen 2.7; 3.19. 2: Authority, v. 4; Gen 1.28; 9.2. 3. His own image, Gen 1.26. 6–7: Knowledge … good and evil, in Genesis these things come to humans as a result of disobedience (Gen 2.4–3.24). Ben Sira ignores that part of the story. 11–14: A shift to Israel and the giving of the Torah. 11: Law of life, the Torah; see 45.5; Deut 30.15–20. 12–13: Glorious majesty … voice, a reference to the Sinai theophany and covenant (Ex 19). 14: Neighbor, Lev 19.18 (cf. Ex 20.13–17; Deut 5.17–21). 15–24: God sees human sin (cf. 15.19) and judges, but almsgiving and repentance allow a return. 17. The Lord's own portion, Deut 32.8–9. 22: Signet ring (Jer 22.24; Hag 2.23) and apple of his eye (Deut 32.10) suggest a special relationship with God.
17.25–32: Mercy and repentance. God's mercy allows repentance. 27–28: The dead in Hades (see 14.12n) cannot praise God (cf., e.g., Pss 6.5; 30.9; 88.10–12; Bar 2.17), which should be humanity's response to God (39.12–35; 43.11,27–33). 30–31: If the sun's light can fail, how much more can flesh and blood. 32: Dust and ashes, see 10.9n.
18.1–14: God's majesty and compassion. Human frailty and mortality prompt God's compassion. 4–7: Cf. 43.27–33. 4: To none, cf. 42.17. 7: Have finished, trying “to fathom the wonders of the Lord” (v. 6). 8: What are human beings, Ps 8.4. 9: Number of days, Ps 90.10. 11: Cf. Ps 103.14. 13: Shepherd, cf. Ps 23.1–4; Isa 40.11; Ezek 34.11–16.
18.15–29: Speech and action. A gracious person speaks kind words and the wise person is cautious in everything. 18: The fool is a frequent foil to the wise person in wisdom literature. 21: Falling ill is related to sin (cf. 38.9–15). 23: Test, cf. Ex 17.2; Deut 6.16. 24: Consideration of the day of death should affect how one acts in life.
18.30–19.17: Self-control and gossip. 31: Laughingstock, that is, exposed to public shame (cf. 6.4; 13.7; 42.11). 19.2: Cf. Prov 31.2–5. 3: Snatched away, will die an untimely death. 10: Let it die with you, self-control means not repeating gossip. 13–17: Do not believe everything you hear (v. 15) but first ask neighbor or friend directly. 17: Law of the Most High, cf. Lev 19.17–18.
19.20–30: Wisdom contrasted with cleverness. 20: Wisdom … fear of the Lord … fulfillment of the law, the basic triad in Sirach; see 1.11–30n. 23: Cleverness (Gk “panourgia”) has a negative connotation, since it is deceitful. 29–30: A person's outward appearance reflects his or her inner nature; cf. 13.25–26.
20.1–31: Topics related to speech. The advice in this section appeals to avoidance of shame. A significant difference between proper and improper speech concerns speaking at the proper time. 4: Sinners cannot be compelled to act properly any more than a eunuch can engage in sexual intercourse. 5–6: Cf. Prov 17.27–28. 9–12: Although the sages argued for moral laws of cause-and-effect, they recognized both ironies and paradoxes; cf. Prov 14.12; 16.25; Eccl 9.1–12. 20: Cf. Prov 26.7. 24–26: On lying, see 7.13; 25.2; Prov 6.17; 12.22; Ps 5.6.
21.1–10: Sin and repentance. Sin can inflict grievous, even mortal, wounds, but forgiveness is possible. 3: Lawlessness, equated with sin, emphasizes the importance of fulfilling the law. 5: Prayer of the poor, cf. 35.17–22a; Deut 15.9. 9: Bundle of tow, highly combustible flax. Fire, punishment in this lifetime rather than after death. 10: Hades, see 14.12n.
21.11–22.18: The wise and the foolish. 12: Clever, here in a positive sense (cf. 19.23). 13–14: Increase like a flood … broken jar, the knowledge of the wise overflows, while the fool's mind can hold nothing (cf. 22.9). 19: Compare the same imagery used positively of Wisdom in 6.24–25,29–30. 22–24: Being educated means having good manners. 22.1: Filthy stone, a rock used for wiping after a bowel movement. 3–6: Son … daughter, sons were valued more highly than daughters in Ben Sira's social world (cf. 7.24–25; 42.9–14), but both can bring shame on their father. 6: Thrashing, corporal punishment was the norm in parenting and education (30.12–13; Prov 13.24; 23.13–14). 9: See 21.13–14. 11: For the dead nothing more exists; for the fool the rest of life is at stake. 12: Seven days, the traditional mourning period, but cf. 38.17. 13: Spattered, the image is of an animal shaking off that affects anyone standing close by. 16–18: Fool's resolve contrasts with the resolve of the wise.
22.19–26: Preserving friendship. See also 6.5–17. Even true and loyal friends might make mistakes that require reconciliation. True friends stand by friends in good and bad times. 25–26: Shelter a friend runs counter to Ben Sira's usual ethic of caution; cf. 11.29.
22.27–23.6. Prayer for self-control. 23.2–3: Whips … discipline, an appeal to the idea that corporal punishment will prevent mistakes, in this case sins. 23.1,4: O Lord, Father, cf. 51.10.
23.7–15: Self-control in speech. This poem connects with the theme of the previous section. 9: On misuse of God's name, see Ex 20.7; Deut 5.11. On oaths, Lev 5.4; Num 3.1–16. 12: Death, perhaps a reference to blasphemy (Lev 24.16). Inheritance of Jacob, the Israelites. 14: Curse the day of your birth, see Job 3.3–5; Jer 20.14–18.
23.16–27. Sexual sins. A poem on sexual license of both men and women. 16: Two kinds … third, a numerical proverb, often used in wisdom literature (cf. 25.1–2,7; 26.5–6,28; 50.25–26; Prov 30.18–19,21–23). 21: Death was the stipulated punishment for adultery (Lev 20.10; Deut 22.22–24). Here the punishment is public but not specified (see also v. 24). 19: Eyes of the Lord, God knows all human actions (cf. 16.17–22). 23: Disobeyed the law, Ex 20.14; Lev 20.10; Deut 5.18; 22.22. 25–26: The adulteress's children will not flourish (cf. Wis 3.16–19; 4.3–6; also Deut 5.9–10). 27: The reference to fear of the Lord and the commandments leads directly to Wisdom's self-praise in ch 24.
24.1–22. Wisdom's self-praise. An aretalogy (first-person praise of oneself, often connected with the Egyptian goddess Isis) in which Wisdom is personified as a woman (Prov 8.22–36; cf. Bar 3.9–4.4; Wis 7.22–8.1) and dwells in the Jerusalem Temple. 1–2: Her people, Israel; the assembly of the Most High, the heavenly host or divine council (cf. Ps 82.1,6–7). 3: From the mouth of the Most High evokes Gen 1. Covered like a mist, cf. Gen 1.2; Wis 7.25. 4: Pillar of cloud, see Ex 13.21–22; 33.9–10; Wis 10.17–18. 6: Wisdom has a universal reach (cf. 1.15). 7: Resting place, Job 28.23; 1 En. 42. 8–12: Wisdom, who was created in the beginning (cf. Gen 1.1), dwells with Israel (cf. Bar 3.36–37) in the Jerusalem Temple. 9: Prov 8.22–23. 10: Holy tent, an allusion to the tabernacle (Ex 25.8–9), which represents the Temple. Ministered, Wisdom functions liturgically as a priest. 12: Portion of the Lord, cf. 17.17. 13–17: A comparison of Wisdom with plants, perfume, and fragrant substances, emphasizing her beauty and expanse and her connection with the Temple. See 50.8–12 where roses, cedar, palm, cypress, and olive describe the high priest Simon II. 13: Lebanon, a mountain range in modern Lebanon and Syria famous for its forests. Hermon, a mountain on the border of modern Israel and Syria. 14: En-gedi and Jericho, oases in the region near the Dead Sea. 15: These ingredients were used in the service of the Temple (Ex 30.22–38), which is infused with Wisdom's presence, just as with incense. 19–22: Wisdom is better than fine food and drink, and those who eat and drink will desire more (cf. 6.19; Prov 9.1–6). 23: Ben Sira is the first to relate Torah and wisdom in this way; see also Bar 4.1.

31.12–32.13. Table manners. Ben Sira's students needed to learn proper manners in order to navigate social occasions. The table of the great, or banquet, was an event where one could garner acclaim and honor or experience embarrassment and shame. Advice about manners is frequent in Egyptian wisdom texts, such as Ptahhotep and Amenemope. 12–18: Proper table manners (cf. Prov 23.1–3). 19–24: Moderation leads to good health. 25–31: On wine drinking and its deleterious effects, see Prov 20.1; 23.29–35; 31.4–5. The background may be the symposium, which featured much eating, drinking, and entertainment. See Philo, On the Contemplative Life 48–63; Letter of Aristeas 187–300. 32.1: The master of the feast was responsible for the needs of those attending (2 Macc 2.27; Letter of Aristeas 182). 7–9: The young were expected to keep silence before elders; cf. Job 29.8; 32.4,6.
32.14–33.6: Deliberate judgments. 14: Rise early, for prayer (see 39.5). 16–17: True judgments, perhaps a reference to the sage in an official capacity (cf. 38.31–33). 33.7–15: The pairs. God created the cosmos in pairs (v. 15), an order that can be observed and extends to humans, as the wise and foolish or the godly and the sinner (v. 14). 7–9: Sabbath and liturgical festivals (cf. Gen 1.14; 2.3). 10: Gen 2.7; 3.19. 12: Blessed and exalted, figures such as Abraham and Moses. Brought near to himself, a likely reference to the priesthood. Some he cursed, perhaps referring to the Canaanites (cf. 16.9). 13: Clay, cf. Isa 29.16; 45.9; 64.8; Jer 18.6.
33.16–19: Ben Sira the sage. For other authorial self-references, see 24.30–34; 34.9–13; 39.12–13; 50.27. 16: Keep vigil, a prophetic image; see Ezek 33.7; Hab 2.1 (cf. Jer 1.12; 31.28; 44.27 for the same verb). A gleaner collects what remains after the harvest; see Deut 24.19–22. 19: Cf. 24.34.
33.20–33: The household. 20–24: Loss of control over property and family brought a stain upon your honor. 25–33: Slavery (cf. 7.20–21) was a sanctioned institution, which was regulated in Israelite law (Ex 21.2–11,20–21,26–27; Lev 25.39–46; Deut 15.12–18). 31: Bought him with blood, the high cost of purchasing a slave justified good treatment. 32: Runs away, cf. Deut 23.15–16.
34.1–8: Dreams. Dreams, divinations, and omens do not provide reliable knowledge. Ben Sira sets these against the law and wisdom (v. 8). He may be opposing the apocalyptic movement, which emphasized knowledge through dreams and visions. 2: Pursues the wind, cf. Eccl 1.14. 3: Dreams are a projection of the dreamer, a view that seems very modern. 4: Clean … unclean, cf. Job 14.4. 6: From the Most High, only divinely inspired dreams are acceptable (cf. 34.9–13: Experience through travel. See 39.4; 51.13.
34.14–20: Fear of the Lord. See 1.11–30; 2.1–18. 14: Spirit … will live, in the sense of being energized, encouraged, not in a postmortem existence. 19: Common images in Israel's hymnic tradition (e.g., Pss 3.3; 7.10; 18.2,17,35; 61.3–4; 91.1–4; 121.3–6).
34.21–35.26. Sacrifice and true worship. Ben Sira supported the Temple and its priesthood (cf. 50.1–24) and advocated sacrificing according to the law. Social justice and ethical behavior must motivate and accompany sacrifice, however. 21: Blemished, cf. Lev 22.18–25. 24–27: Ben Sira connects material resources with the ability to live (cf. 29.15; 31.4). Thus, taking what little the poor have constitutes murder. 30: Touching a corpse makes one ritually impure, which is alleviated through washing (Num 19.10–20). 31: Fasts, perhaps a reference to the Day of Atonement (Lev 23.27–32).
35.1–13: Keeping the law, heeding the commandments, giving alms all atone for sin like sacrifice; cf. 3.3,14,30. God looks favorably on sacrifices made willingly and generously. 2: Offering of well-being, cf. Leviticus 3. 3: Choice flour, cf. Leviticus 2. 4: Thank offering, cf. Lev 7.12–21. 6: Empty-handed, see Ex 23.15; Deut 16.16. 10: First fruits, see Ex 23.16,19; 34.22,26; Lev 2.14; 23.10,17. 11: Tithe, one-tenth of one's produce and harvest, dedicated to God (cf. Deut 14.22–29).
35.14–26: Ben Sira's claim that God is just leads to an indictment of unrighteous individuals generally and the nations specifically, which forms the transition to the prayer of 36.1–22. Ben Sira may be alluding to the political situation of his time. 17: On divine concern for the vulnerable orphan and widow, see, e.g., Deut 10.17–18; Ps 68.5.
36.1–22: Prayer for the deliverance of Israel. A prayer of lament for deliverance from foreign nations, perhaps a reference to Seleucid rulers. 4–7: A series of allusions to the Exodus. 4: The events of the Exodus convinced the Egyptians that God is the Lord; see Ex 7.17; 8.22; 14.4. 6: Signs … wonders … hand … right arm, see Ex 7.3; 11.9–10; 15.6; Deut 4.34; 7.19. 10: The Hebrew words day (Heb “qets”) and appointed time (Heb “moʿed”) occur frequently in Jewish eschatological literature. 13: A widespread prophetic hope that God would gather the dispersed of Israel (cf. Firstborn, Ex 4.22. 18: Your dwelling, God's presence was thought to reside in the Temple; see 1 Kings 8.12; Ps 132.14. 20–21: Fulfill the prophecies reflects a developing view of prophecy in this period as prediction of future events (cf. 1QpHab 7.1–5).
36.23–37.15: Choosing wives, friends, and counselors. 26: One girl is preferable, that is, as the man's choice of a wife. 29: A helper fit for him, see Gen 2.18. 30: A fugitive and a wanderer, like Cain (Gen 4.12,14). 37.1–6: On friends, see 6.15–17; 9.10–16; 19.13–17; 22.19–26; 27.16–21. 3: Inclination to evil, Heb “yetser”; see 15.14n. 11: Her rival, perhaps another wife (26.6).
37.16–31: Deliberative speech and moderation. 17–18: Cf. 5.13. 26: The themes of achieving honor and having immortality through one's name run through Sirach (e.g., 10.28–31; 15.6; 33.23; 39.9–11; 40.19; 41.11–13). 27–31: Cf. 31.12–31.
38.1–15: Illness and physicians. While Ben Sira recommends treatment by a physician, he reaffirms the traditional idea that illness results from sin. 5: Water made sweet, see Ex 15.23–25. 9–15: On sin as a cause of illness, see 18.19–21; Deut 28.21–22,27,35; Jn 9.2; and Job 10; 33–34 on the relationship between sin and suffering. 11: On generosity in sacrifice, see 35.10–11.
38.16–23: Mourning. 16: Do not neglect the burial, proper burial was a religious obligation (cf. Tob 1.17–18; 4.3–4; 6.15; 12.12; 14.12–13). 17: One day, or two, a much shorter period than stipulated in 22.12.
38.24–39.11: The trades and the scribe. Ancient scribes/sages like Ben Sira were intellectuals and public figures, not only producers of documents. The contrast between the sage and artisans, farmers, and tradespersons was popular in Egyptian wisdom, such as the Satire of the Trades (the Instruction of Kheti), but Ben Sira has a more positive attitude toward these occupations than the Egyptian text. 24: Leisure, artisans and tradespersons are too busy to take the time for study. 27: Signets of seals were used to make impressions that authorized identity, much as a personal signature functions today. 32–34: Artisans, farmers, and tradespersons maintain the fabric of the world, but the scribe enables society to function. 39.4: Cf. 34.12–13. 6: Filled with the spirit of understanding, the scribe's wisdom comes through study (38.34c–39.3) and revelatory inspiration. 9–11: The wise scribe will live on through his memory and name; cf. 37.26; 44.10–15.
39.12–35: Praise of God's creation. A hymn of praise (vv. 16–31) with an introduction (vv. 12–15) and a personal conclusion (32–35). 12: Like the full moon, Ben Sira's teaching illuminates (cf. 24.32). 13–14: Reminiscent of Wisdom in 24.13–17. 16: Very good, cf. Gen 1.4,10,12,18,21,25,31. Appointed time, see 36.10; 39.34; 48.10; Eccl 3.11. 17: The waters stood in a heap (cf. Ex 15.8; Josh 3.16). At the word of his mouth, cf. Gen 1.9–10; Ps 33.6. 23: Watered land into salt, Sodom and Gomorrah (cf. Gen 19.24–26). 25–27: Though the good share the good things of creation, sinners have both good things and bad. Yet the good things in creation turn to evils for sinners. 28–31: Elements created to punish the wicked. 29: Created for vengeance, cf. Job 38.23.

An explication of Ben Sira's views expressed in 39.22–31 about the righteous and unrighteous. 1: Yoke, a positive and negative image in Sirach; cf. 6.23–31; 26.7; 28.19–20; 30.13; 33.27; 51.26. Adam, see Gen 3.17–19. Lk 16.3). 41.3–4: Death's decree … for all, see 14.12,17; Gen 3.19. Hades, see 14.12n. 5–10: The main thought concerns disgrace or shame. The most extensive teaching on shame in Sirach (cf. 4.20–28), here differentiating between things that bring inappropriate shame and things of which to be appropriately ashamed. 14–16: An introduction summarizing the theme. 17–18: Be ashamed … before, shows the public nature of honor and shame. 42.1: Find favor, the end result of proper shame is honor. 2: The law and the covenant head the list of things not to be ashamed of. 42.9–14: Daughters. Deut 22.13–29.) 11: A man's inability to control important culturally significant aspects of his life, here daughters, brings shame. 13: From a woman, perhaps a reference to Eve (cf. 25.24). 14: The most pernicious articulation of Ben Sira's misogyny (cf. 25.13–26; 36.26–31).

49.1–13: Josiah and succeeding ancestors. 2: 2 Kings 23.4–25. 4–7: A summary of the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 586 bce. As in the Deuteronomistic History, only these three kings were fully righteous. 6: Jeremiah had foretold, Jer 7.12–15. 7: See Jer 1.5,9–10. 8: Ezek 1.4–14; 10. 9: See textual note g and cf. Ezek 14.14,20. 10: Ben Sira knows the Twelve Minor Prophets (Hosea–Malachi) as one book; cf. 46.12. 11–13: Postexilic leaders: Zerubbabel (Ezra 3.2; Hag 2.23), Jeshua (Ezra 3.2; Hag 1.12; Zech 3; 6.9–13), Nehemiah (Nehemiah, esp. 2.17–3.32).
49.14–16: Concluding ancestors. A conclusion that extends back to the first human. 14: Enoch, see 44.16n. 15: Joseph, see Gen 39–50, esp. 50.25–26; Ex 13.19. 16: Shem (Gen 9.18), Seth (Gen 5.3), Enosh (Gen 5.6–11), and Adam (Gen 1.26–31).
50.1–24: The priest Simon II. The son of Onias was high priest from 219 bce to 196 bce. Ben Sira emphasizes his dual roles as ruler and priest through a theme of glory. 1–4: Simon's public works projects; cf. those of Hezekiah (48.17) and Nehemiah (49.13). 5–11: The language recalls that of Wisdom in ch 24 and the heavenly bodies in ch 43. 5: House of the curtain, the Temple. 12–21: A detailed description of the Temple ritual and sacrifices, either for the Day of Atonement (Lev 16) or the daily offering (45.14; Num 28.1–8). 16: Trumpets, see Num 10.10. 20: Blessing of the Lord, see Lev 9.22–23; Num 6.22–27. 22–24: A concluding benediction.
50.25–29: Epilogue. 25–26: Seir (Edom), the Philistines, and Shechem (the Samaritans) were traditional enemies of Israel. 27: The author gives his name, probably Yeshua ben Eleazar ben Sira. Poured forth wisdom, cf. 24.23–34.
51.1–12: A hymn of thanksgiving. This hymn has many linguistic reminiscences of the Psalms. 5,6: Hades, see 14.12n. 10: Father, see 23.1,4. 12: After v. 12, the medieval Hebrew manuscripts insert a hymn based on Ps 136.
51.13–30. Ben Sira's search for Wisdom. An acrostic poem in the first person on the sage's search for Wisdom, who is represented as a woman. A different and more erotic version can be found in 11QPsaa. 13: Travels, cf. 34.11; 39.4. 14: Wisdom is connected with the Temple as in 24.10. 23: House of instruction, a wisdom “school,” probably in Ben Sira's own house. 26: Bearing a yoke can be positive or negative in Sirach; see 6.23–31; 26.7; 28.19–20; 30.13; 33.27; 40.1. 28: Getting Wisdom through Ben Sira's teaching is connected with acquisition of wealth.
