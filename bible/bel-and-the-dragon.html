Bel and the Dragon

Name and Location in Canon

The story of “Bel and the Dragon” belongs to the Additions to Daniel that are preserved in the Septuagint but not in the MT of Daniel. The English word “dragon” is derived from Gk “drakōn,” which can also mean “serpent” or “snake.” The story itself seems to be describing rituals involving sacred serpents. Its placement varies in the Septuagint. It appears as ch 13 in Theodotion's version of Daniel, which is the version printed here, but in some manuscripts of the OG version the story appears as ch 14, following Susanna.

Composition and Language

Although it is not possible to identify the authors, “Bel and the Dragon” seems to have been composed independently and later incorporated into the growing collection of Daniel stories found in the Septuagint. It was probably written in a Semitic language, either Aramaic or Hebrew, and later translated into Greek. Differences between the Old Greek version of the story and Theodotion's version may indicate that the OG reflects an older and independent tradition about Daniel that existed before the Hebrew/Aramaic text of Daniel became authoritative. A translator probably removed the information about Daniel as a priest, found in the OG, and inserted the Persian king Cyrus in place of the king of Babylon in the Theodotionic version in order to integrate the story into the larger collection of Daniel stories. The similarities between this story and Dan 3 and 6 may indicate that it was composed in the Persian period (539–333 bce) when court tales enjoyed wide popularity throughout the Persian empire, though the story could also date from the Hellenistic period. Daniel's destruction of the temple of Bel may also hint at the historical destruction of the temple of Bel by Persian king Xerxes I who ruled from 486–465 bce. If so, the story could not be earlier than Xerxes's reign.

Interpretation

The story contains two separate incidents concerning idolatry. The chapter uses the recurring motifs of food, feasting, and eating to tie these incidents together and to highlight reversals in the plot. In each incident, Daniel boldly exposes the folly of worshiping idols and openly ridicules Babylonian idols (see also Isa 46), a stance that one does not find in Dan 1–6. Although his denigration of the Babylonian gods alienates him from the Babylonian priests, his cleverness and fidelity bring honor to him and to the God of the Jews. The story has many comic elements, but the king's erratic behavior and the anger of the people put Daniel's life at risk. As in Dan 3 and 6, the wise man is ultimately rescued through the sovereign power of God, which the pagan king finally recognizes.

Amy C. Merrill Willis

1When King Astyages was laid to rest with his ancestors, Cyrus the Persian succeeded to his kingdom. 2Daniel was a companion of the king, and was the most honored of all his Friends.

3Now the Babylonians had an idol called Bel, and every day they provided for it twelve bushels of choice flour and forty sheep and six measuresa of wine. 4The king revered it and went every day to worship it. But Daniel worshiped his own God.

So the king said to him, “Why do you not worship Bel?” 5He answered, “Because I do not revere idols made with hands, but the living God, who created heaven and earth and has dominion over all living creatures.”

6The king said to him, “Do you not think that Bel is a living god? Do you not see how much he eats and drinks every day?” 7And Daniel laughed, and said, “Do not be deceived, O king, for this thing is only clay inside and bronze outside, and it never ate or drank anything.”

8Then the king was angry and called the priests of Belb and said to them, “If you do not tell me who is eating these provisions, you shall die. 9But if you prove that Bel is eating them, Daniel shall die, because he has spoken blasphemy against Bel.” Daniel said to the king, “Let it be done as you have said.”

10Now there were seventy priests of Bel, besides their wives and children. So the king went with Daniel into the temple of Bel. 11The priests of Bel said, “See, we are now going outside; you yourself, O king, set out the food and prepare the wine, and shut the door and seal it with your signet. 12When you return in the morning, if you do not find that Bel has eaten it all, we will die; otherwise Daniel will, who is telling lies about us.” 13They were unconcerned, for beneath the table they had made a hidden entrance, through which they used to go in regularly and consume the provisions. 14After they had gone out, the king set out the food for Bel. Then Daniel ordered his servants to bring ashes, and they scattered them throughout the whole temple in the presence of the king alone. Then they went out, shut the door and sealed it with the king's signet, and departed. 15During the night the priests came as usual, with their wives and children, and they ate and drank everything.

16Early in the morning the king rose and came, and Daniel with him. 17The king said, “Are the seals unbroken, Daniel?” He answered, “They are unbroken, O king.” 18As soon as the doors were opened, the king looked at the table, and shouted in a loud voice, “You are great, O Bel, and in you there is no deceit at all!”

19But Daniel laughed and restrained the king from going in. “Look at the floor,” he said, “and notice whose footprints these are.” 20The king said, “I see the footprints of men and women and children.”

21Then the king was enraged, and he arrested the priests and their wives and children. They showed him the secret doors through which they used to enter to consume what was on the table. 22Therefore the king put them to death, and gave Bel over to Daniel, who destroyed it and its temple.

23Now in that placea there was a great dragon, which the Babylonians revered. 24The king said to Daniel, “You cannot deny that this is a living god; so worship him.” 25Daniel said, “I worship the Lord my God, for he is the living God. 26But give me permission, O king, and I will kill the dragon without sword or club.” The king said, “I give you permission.”

27Then Daniel took pitch, fat, and hair, and boiled them together and made cakes, which he fed to the dragon. The dragon ate them, and burst open. Then Daniel said, “See what you have been worshiping!”

28When the Babylonians heard about it, they were very indignant and conspired against the king, saying, “The king has become a Jew; he has destroyed Bel, and killed the dragon, and slaughtered the priests.” 29Going to the king, they said, “Hand Daniel over to us, or else we will kill you and your household.” 30The king saw that they were pressing him hard, and under compulsion he handed Daniel over to them.

31They threw Daniel into the lions’ den, and he was there for six days. 32There were seven lions in the den, and every day they had been given two human bodies and two sheep; but now they were given nothing, so that they would devour Daniel.

33Now the prophet Habakkuk was in Judea; he had made a stew and had broken bread into a bowl, and was going into the field to take it to the reapers. 34But the angel of the Lord said to Habakkuk, “Take the food that you have to Babylon, to Daniel, in the lions’ den.” 35Habakkuk said, “Sir, I have never seen Babylon, and I know nothing about the den.” 36Then the angel of the Lord took him by the crown of his head and carried him by his hair; with the speed of the windb he set him down in Babylon, right over the den.

37Then Habakkuk shouted, “Daniel, Daniel! Take the food that God has sent you.” 38Daniel said, “You have remembered me, O God, and have not forsaken those who love you.” 39So Daniel got up and ate. And the angel of God immediately returned Habakkuk to his own place.

40On the seventh day the king came to mourn for Daniel. When he came to the den he looked in, and there sat Daniel! 41The king shouted with a loud voice, “You are great, O Lord, the God of Daniel, and there is no other besides you!” 42Then he pulled Daniela out, and threw into the den those who had attempted his destruction, and they were instantly eaten before his eyes.

1–2: Daniel in Persia. 1: Astyages was the king of Media (585–550 bce) until defeated by his grandson, Cyrus the Persian (Dan 6.28), who later conquered Babylon. 2: Most honored, see Dan 2.48. Friends, high-ranking members of the royal court (1 Macc 2.18).
3–7: The worship of Bel. 3: Bel, an epithet for the Babylonian creator god, Marduk; see Jer 50.2; Isa 46.1. The Greek historian Herodotus (Hist. 1.183) writes of a massive gold statue in the temple of Bel during the time of Cyrus that had been revered by Nebuchadnezzar. Twelve bushels … forty sheep, the figures are exaggerated, but the custom of providing offerings to sacred images is well documented. In practice, the image “consumed” the offerings by looking at them; the king and the priests would receive the remaining food. 4–5: The king must be educated about the true God; see also Dan 3.6; 6.21. 6: The king naively believes that the image consumes all the food, thus emphasizing the foolishness of idol worship. 7: Clay … and bronze, see Dan 2.33; Isa 40.18–19; 46.6.
8–22: Bel on trial. 8–9: The king's volatility resembles Dan 2.5–12 and 3.13–15. The threat of death is a folklore convention that heightens dramatic tension. 11: Seal it with your signet, Dan 6.17. 12: Telling lies about us, Daniel's ridicule of Bel, not professional rivalry, leads to the priests’ anger and false accusations (see Dan 3.12; 6.2–5). 13–14: Daniel outwits the priests and exposes their deception with his own cleverness rather than divine help. This kind of scene, “the mystery of the locked room,” later becomes a conventional one in detective stories. 16: Early in the morning, see also Dan 6.19. 18: In you there is no deceit, the ironic exclamation emphasizes the king's gullibility. 22: The king put them to death, see Dan 6.24n; Sus 62. Gave Bel over to Daniel, Daniel is not only vindicated, but is allowed to carry out an act of iconoclasm. This plot turn has no parallel in the other tales.
23–27: The dragon. 23: Dragon is synonymous with serpent and can also refer to watery monsters such as Leviathan (Ps 74; Isa 27.1). Although serpents could be used as symbols of divine power in the ancient Near East (see 2 Kings 18.4), there is no independent evidence that the Babylonians worshipped live snakes. 24: In contrast to Bel, the king argues that the dragon is a living god because he does eat. 26: The focus and function of the story is idol polemics, so Daniel is shown to be openly critical of the serpent worship. 27: It is unclear how pitch, fat, and hair could cause the serpent to explode. The motif of eating ties the story to the larger cycle of tales by developing contrasts between Daniel's restricted diet (Dan 1), which allows him to prosper, and the eating that brings death to the dragon; see also Belshazzar's deadly feasting in Dan 5.
28–42: Daniel in the lion's den. 28: The king has become a Jew is an exaggeration, but it anticipates v. 40. It is also a dangerous accusation for a king who should, for political reasons, prefer the native religion of his land. Such statements of conversion begin to appear in Jewish literature during the second century bce, see Jdt 14.10; 2 Macc 9.17. 29: In a reversal of plot, the king, who threatened the priests with death in v. 8, is now being threatened by the people who wish him to hand Daniel over. 31: Lions’ den … for six days, this version of Daniel in the pit of lions is more developed than that in Dan 6.16–24. 33–39: The story shifts focus to the prophet Habakkuk, evoking the larger tradition of prophetic feeding stories (1 Kings 17.7–16; 2 Kings 4.38–44). God intervenes miraculously to feed Daniel, not to shut the lions’ mouths (cf. Dan 6.22); the lions’ hunger is apparently not a problem. 40: The seventh day, cf. Dan 6.18–19. 41: You are great, O Lord …, cf. v. 18. The king asserts God's unique sovereignty (cf. Dan 3.28; 4.3,34–35; 6.26–27; Isa 45.13–14,21). 42: In an ironic turn, the lions instantly eat those who had tried to kill Daniel; cf. Dan 6.24.
