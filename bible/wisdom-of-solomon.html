Wisdom of Solomon

Name and Authorship

The Wisdom of Solomon is the title of the book in the Septuagint (LXX), the Greek translation of the Jewish scriptures. In the Latin Vulgate it is simply called the Book of Wisdom. As in Ecclesiastes, Song of Solomon, and parts of Proverbs, the implied speaker is King Solomon, renowned for the wisdom granted to him by God in answer to his prayer (1 Kings 3.8; Wis 9.8). But the name Solomon never appears in the book apart from the title. Already for Origen (ca. 185–254 ce) and Augustine (354–430 ce) Solomonic authorship was considered improbable. Nonetheless, several types of writing the author employs are identifiable, including Aristotelian genres and styles of writing such as protreptic (instruction or persuasion), epideictic (praise or blame), the encomium (praise), and synkrisis (comparison by juxtaposition). These features point to a Hellenistic-Jewish context.

The anonymous author was likely a well-educated Hellenized Jew associated with one of the synagogues in Alexandria during the Roman occupation of Egypt (ca. 30 bce–40 ce). The book was meant to bolster the faith, especially of young Jews who felt pressure from the culture of Hellenism and the power of Rome, and who were discouraged by their limited possibilities to thrive in an increasingly antagonistic culture. The ideas presented in the book would provide arguments for Jews to defend and articulate their faith to themselves and perhaps to their Greek and Egyptian neighbors.

Canonical Status

The main authoritative texts used in every section and argument of the book originate in the LXX translation of the Hebrew scriptures. Though the first part of the book (chs 1–6) displays effective use of Hebrew parallelism, the Wisdom of Solomon was originally written in Greek. Already Origen expressed uncertainty about its canonical status, a doubt emphasized by Jerome (ca. 340–420 ce) who preferred the shorter Hebrew canon of scripture. The main champion for its canonical status in the Christian canon was Augustine, who argued for its divine inspiration based on the venerable use of the book in the liturgy, especially in funeral rites: “The souls of the righteous are in the hand of God, and no torment will ever touch them” (3.1), a text often chosen for funeral readings even today because of its powerful expression of hope.

The book is canonical in the Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic churches but is considered one of the Apocrypha by Protestant churches. No manuscript of the Wisdom of Solomon has been found among the Dead Sea Scrolls.

Structure and Interpretation

The book has three parts, each with a particularly focused argument:

1.1–6.21 Exhortation to love justice in order to avoid death and to gain immortality;
6.22–11.1 Praise of divine wisdom, which makes one friends with God;
11.2–19.22 The Exodus from Egypt and slavery, as an example of the providence of God.

Because of differences in style in the three parts of the book, a few scholars have proposed composite authorship. However, the Greek genres of protreptic and epideictic writing allow for different styles to be used in a single work. Moreover, the thread of the argument, which runs through all three sections of the book, makes an integrated line of reasoning for Jews to remain faithful to the law and the covenant against growing cultural and religious opposition in Alexandria.

In the first section the “two ways” of a life of justice and injustice are juxtaposed, as frequently in wisdom literature. The argument in favor of justice is developed metaphorically as a juridical trial scene (accusation, defense, counteraccusation, examination of the evidence, confession of guilt, judgment). Many of the images employed in this section originate from Isaiah 52–58 (the “suffering servant,” the barren woman, the eunuch, the just, divine judgment). The impious are accused of a life of injustice, which leads to death (ch 1). The imaginary voice of the impious defends a life of pleasure, power, and violence by attacking the hope of the righteous as vacuous (ch 2). Through a series of comparisons and contrasts the author constructs diptychs for the righteous and the impious where appearances and reality are evaluated to establish the final outcome in each case. The way of righteousness leads to immortality, whereas the path of injustice leads to death (chs 3–4). The clearest presentation of the belief in life after death in the Hebrew Bible occurs in the Wisdom of Solomon (ch 3). Dramatically confirming the author's reasoning, the impious—when they see the final state of life of the just one who suffered a tragic death at their hands—confess their wrongdoing and the emptiness of their own unjust project (5.1–14). Divine judgment follows: in an apocalyptic scene, the righteous will live in peace with God, whereas the impious are blown away to oblivion (5.15–23). The forces of God's creation in the cosmos are presented as coming to the help of the just and thwarting the designs of the impious, a theme that recurs in the second and third parts of the book. The final exhortation encourages kings and readers alike to desire wisdom in order to avoid the pitfalls of injustice and embrace a life of justice which brings immortality (6.1–21).

The second section describes the cosmos as pervaded by the wisdom of God, who created all things through divine providence, providing a positive view of the forces of creation. The wisdom of God enables humans to be practitioners of justice and therefore to receive the gift of immortality. Only here in the book does the speech turn to the first person (6.22). The speaker is the unnamed King Solomon who, upon coming to the throne as a young man, beseeches God for a discerning mind (1 Kings 3.8). As in Prov 8.22–30 and Sir 24, wisdom is personified as a woman whom Solomon desires to be his wife and teacher. In his prayer for wisdom, Solomon describes her as sitting beside the throne of God and having been present during the creation of the cosmos (ch 9). Since she knows intimately the realms of the divine and the cosmos, she can help humans become friends of God (7.27). This is the most Greek section of the book, where philosophical ideas and tropes appear, such as the four virtues of Plato and the literary device of aretalogy, which praises a deity's miraculous deeds. In praising wisdom, the author describes seven pairs of characters in which each pair has one who is saved by wisdom and another who perishes for lack of wisdom. Moving from Adam to Moses, the author covers the entire book of Genesis and concludes by introducing the main theme of the final section of the book, the Exodus from Egypt.

In the third section, events described in Exodus and Numbers illustrate how the providence of God comes to the help of the righteous. The Exodus from Egypt is the primary example. Comparison and contrast of the righteous Israelites and the impious Egyptians echoes the struggle between the just and the godless in the first part of the book. Although in the opening exhortation to justice the metaphorical trial provides a vision of a future eschatological judgment, the story of the Exodus shows how God came to the aid of the righteous in the past, demonstrating that the future judgment is not an illusory hope.

This treatment of biblical narrative constructs two novel viewpoints for understanding the significance of the ten plagues of Exodus. First, the author associates the nature of punishment with the nature of the sin (11.6) in five diptychs:

1. The godless drowned newborn infants in the Nile, and the Nile was turned to blood and became undrinkable (11.6–14); in turn God gives water in the desert;
2. The godless worshiped animals; in turn animals plague the godless (11.15–16.14);
3. The godless refused to recognize the true God of heaven and earth; in turn, the forces of heaven, rain and hail, destroy the crops of the godless (16.15–29);
4. The godless enslaved the righteous; in turn the godless are enslaved by darkness (17.1–18.4);
5. The godless killed infants in the river; in turn the firstborn of the godless are killed in the tenth plague (18.5–25), and the army of the godless is destroyed by the waters of the Red Sea (19.1–9).

The second perspective on the plagues shows how God used the very same forces of creation that punish the godless to come to the aid of the righteous (11.5,13). Seven contrasts of the plagues on the ungodly Egyptians with the blessings of the righteous Israelites are used to illustrate this point.

Plagues against the ungodly Blessings for the righteous
11.6–14 undrinkable water God gives water in desert
16.1–4 animals suppress the appetite God gives quails to the righteous
16.5–14 animals kill God gives a bronze serpent that saves
16.15–29 creation destroys by fire God provides manna that resists burning
17.1–18.4 captivity by darkness God provides a pillar of fire in the darkness
18.5–25 death of the firstborn Aaron stops the angel of death
19.1–9 drowning in the sea God's people journey on dry land

More generally, the story of the plagues allows the author to reflect on God's mercy and justice (11.17–12.27). Even the manner of punishments is meant to bring the unrighteous to conversion and to teach the righteous to be merciful in turn. A second theological reflection stems from the issue of false worship. The unrighteous worship of animals and idols leads to a critique of all forms of pantheism, idolatry, and animal worship (chs 13–15).

A significant shift of perspective in this third part of the book occurs almost imperceptibly. Throughout the second part, wisdom is described as the prime actor who helps the righteous (ch 10). In the historical reflection on the Exodus, however, God becomes the dramatic actor (chs 11–19). Wisdom is not an entity separate from God. Even in the second part, wisdom is presented as God's manner of sustaining the cosmos and engaging with human beings. Wisdom pervades the cosmos and makes humans friends with God and prophets (7.14,27). In the third part, which explores the significance of the plague narrative for the righteous, the cosmos, which is pervaded by wisdom, becomes a concrete example of God's providence. The very elements of the cosmos aid the righteous, even as they thwart the wicked. Finally, the third part exhibits a different style of writing from the first two. Here, for the first time, is direct speech to God (11.4). The final historical reflection on the Exodus turns into a prayer of thanksgiving to God for the marvelous ways in which God forever seeks to thwart the power of injustice and to bring life for the righteousness (19.22).

1

Love righteousness, you rulers of the earth,

think of the Lord in goodness

and seek him with sincerity of heart;

2because he is found by those who do not put him to the test,

and manifests himself to those who do not distrust him.

3For perverse thoughts separate people from God,

and when his power is tested, it exposes the foolish;

4because wisdom will not enter a deceitful soul,

or dwell in a body enslaved to sin.

5For a holy and disciplined spirit will flee from deceit,

and will leave foolish thoughts behind,

and will be ashamed at the approach of unrighteousness.

6For wisdom is a kindly spirit,

but will not free blasphemers from the guilt of their words;

because God is witness of their inmost feelings,

and a true observer of their hearts, and a hearer of their tongues.

7Because the spirit of the Lord has filled the world,

and that which holds all things together knows what is said,

8therefore those who utter unrighteous things will not escape notice,

and justice, when it punishes, will not pass them by.

9For inquiry will be made into the counsels of the ungodly,

and a report of their words will come to the Lord,

to convict them of their lawless deeds;

10because a jealous ear hears all things,

and the sound of grumbling does not go unheard.

11Beware then of useless grumbling,

and keep your tongue from slander;

because no secret word is without result,a

and a lying mouth destroys the soul.

12Do not invite death by the error of your life,

or bring on destruction by the works of your hands;

13because God did not make death,

and he does not delight in the death of the living.

14For he created all things so that they might exist;

the generative forcesb of the world are wholesome,

and there is no destructive poison in them,

and the dominionc of Hades is not on earth.

15For righteousness is immortal.

16But the ungodly by their words and deeds summoned death;d

considering him a friend, they pined away

and made a covenant with him,

because they are fit to belong to his company.

2

For they reasoned unsoundly, saying to themselves,

“Short and sorrowful is our life,

and there is no remedy when a life comes to its end,

and no one has been known to return from Hades.

2For we were born by mere chance,

and hereafter we shall be as though we had never been,

for the breath in our nostrils is smoke,

and reason is a spark kindled by the beating of our hearts;

3when it is extinguished, the body will turn to ashes,

and the spirit will dissolve like empty air.

4Our name will be forgotten in time,

and no one will remember our works;

our life will pass away like the traces of a cloud,

and be scattered like mist

that is chased by the rays of the sun

and overcome by its heat.

5For our allotted time is the passing of a shadow,

and there is no return from our death,

because it is sealed up and no one turns back.

6“Come, therefore, let us enjoy the good things that exist,

and make use of the creation to the full as in youth.

7Let us take our fill of costly wine and perfumes,

and let no flower of spring pass us by.

8Let us crown ourselves with rosebuds before they wither.

9Let none of us fail to share in our revelry;

everywhere let us leave signs of enjoyment,

because this is our portion, and this our lot.

10Let us oppress the righteous poor man;

let us not spare the widow

or regard the gray hairs of the aged.

11But let our might be our law of right,

for what is weak proves itself to be useless.

12“Let us lie in wait for the righteous man,

because he is inconvenient to us and opposes our actions;

he reproaches us for sins against the law,

and accuses us of sins against our training.

13He professes to have knowledge of God,

and calls himself a childa of the Lord.

14He became to us a reproof of our thoughts;

15the very sight of him is a burden to us,

because his manner of life is unlike that of others,

and his ways are strange.

16We are considered by him as something base,

and he avoids our ways as unclean;

he calls the last end of the righteous happy,

and boasts that God is his father.

17Let us see if his words are true,

and let us test what will happen at the end of his life;

18for if the righteous man is God's child, he will help him,

and will deliver him from the hand of his adversaries.

19Let us test him with insult and torture,

so that we may find out how gentle he is,

and make trial of his forbearance.

20Let us condemn him to a shameful death,

for, according to what he says, he will be protected.”

21Thus they reasoned, but they were led astray,

for their wickedness blinded them,

22and they did not know the secret purposes of God,

nor hoped for the wages of holiness,

nor discerned the prize for blameless souls;

23for God created us for incorruption,

and made us in the image of his own eternity,b

24but through the devil's envy death entered the world,

and those who belong to his company experience it.

3

But the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God,

and no torment will ever touch them.

2In the eyes of the foolish they seemed to have died,

and their departure was thought to be a disaster,

3and their going from us to be their destruction;

but they are at peace.

4For though in the sight of others they were punished,

their hope is full of immortality.

5Having been disciplined a little, they will receive great good,

because God tested them and found them worthy of himself;

6like gold in the furnace he tried them,

and like a sacrificial burnt offering he accepted them.

7In the time of their visitation they will shine forth,

and will run like sparks through the stubble.

8They will govern nations and rule over peoples,

and the Lord will reign over them forever.

9Those who trust in him will understand truth,

and the faithful will abide with him in love,

because grace and mercy are upon his holy ones,

and he watches over his elect.a

10But the ungodly will be punished as their reasoning deserves,

those who disregarded the righteousb

and rebelled against the Lord;

11for those who despise wisdom and instruction are miserable.

Their hope is vain, their labors are unprofitable,

and their works are useless.

12Their wives are foolish, and their children evil;

13their offspring are accursed.

For blessed is the barren woman who is undefiled,

who has not entered into a sinful union;

she will have fruit when God examines souls.

14Blessed also is the eunuch whose hands have done no lawless deed,

and who has not devised wicked things against the Lord;

for special favor will be shown him for his faithfulness,

and a place of great delight in the temple of the Lord.

15For the fruit of good labors is renowned,

and the root of understanding does not fail.

16But children of adulterers will not come to maturity,

and the offspring of an unlawful union will perish.

17Even if they live long they will be held of no account,

and finally their old age will be without honor.

18If they die young, they will have no hope

and no consolation on the day of judgment.

19For the end of an unrighteous generation is grievous.

4

Better than this is childlessness with virtue,

for in the memory of virtuea is immortality,

because it is known both by God and by mortals.

2When it is present, people imitateb it,

and they long for it when it has gone;

throughout all time it marches, crowned in triumph,

victor in the contest for prizes that are undefiled.

3But the prolific brood of the ungodly will be of no use,

and none of their illegitimate seedlings will strike a deep root

or take a firm hold.

4For even if they put forth boughs for a while,

standing insecurely they will be shaken by the wind,

and by the violence of the winds they will be uprooted.

5The branches will be broken off before they come to maturity,

and their fruit will be useless,

not ripe enough to eat, and good for nothing.

6For children born of unlawful unions

are witnesses of evil against their parents when God examines them.c

7But the righteous, though they die early, will be at rest.

8For old age is not honored for length of time,

or measured by number of years;

9but understanding is gray hair for anyone,

and a blameless life is ripe old age.

10There were some who pleased God and were loved by him,

and while living among sinners were taken up.

11They were caught up so that evil might not change their understanding

or guile deceive their souls.

12For the fascination of wickedness obscures what is good,

and roving desire perverts the innocent mind.

13Being perfected in a short time, they fulfilled long years;

14for their souls were pleasing to the Lord,

therefore he took them quickly from the midst of wickedness.

15Yet the peoples saw and did not understand,

or take such a thing to heart,

that God's grace and mercy are with his elect,

and that he watches over his holy ones.

16The righteous who have died will condemn the ungodly who are living,

and youth that is quickly perfectedd will condemn the prolonged old age of the unrighteous.

17For they will see the end of the wise,

and will not understand what the Lord purposed for them,

and for what he kept them safe.

18The unrighteousa will see, and will have contempt for them,

but the Lord will laugh them to scorn.

After this they will become dishonored corpses,

and an outrage among the dead forever;

19because he will dash them speechless to the ground,

and shake them from the foundations;

they will be left utterly dry and barren,

and they will suffer anguish,

and the memory of them will perish.

20They will come with dread when their sins are reckoned up,

and their lawless deeds will convict them to their face.

5

Then the righteous will stand with great confidence

in the presence of those who have oppressed them

and those who make light of their labors.

2When the unrighteousb see them, they will be shaken with dreadful fear,

and they will be amazed at the unexpected salvation of the righteous.

3They will speak to one another in repentance,

and in anguish of spirit they will groan, and say,

4“These are persons whom we once held in derision

and made a byword of reproach—fools that we were!

We thought that their lives were madness

and that their end was without honor.

5Why have they been numbered among the children of God?

And why is their lot among the saints?

6So it was we who strayed from the way of truth,

and the light of righteousness did not shine on us,

and the sun did not rise upon us.

7We took our fill of the paths of lawlessness and destruction,

and we journeyed through trackless deserts,

but the way of the Lord we have not known.

8What has our arrogance profited us?

And what good has our boasted wealth brought us?

9“All those things have vanished like a shadow,

and like a rumor that passes by;

10like a ship that sails through the billowy water,

and when it has passed no trace can be found,

no track of its keel in the waves;

11or as, when a bird flies through the air,

no evidence of its passage is found;

the light air, lashed by the beat of its pinions

and pierced by the force of its rushing flight,

is traversed by the movement of its wings,

and afterward no sign of its coming is found there;

12or as, when an arrow is shot at a target,

the air, thus divided, comes together at once,

so that no one knows its pathway.

13So we also, as soon as we were born, ceased to be,

and we had no sign of virtue to show,

but were consumed in our wickedness.”

14Because the hope of the ungodly is like thistledowna carried by the wind,

and like a light frostb driven away by a storm;

it is dispersed like smoke before the wind,

and it passes like the remembrance of a guest who stays but a day.

15But the righteous live forever,

and their reward is with the Lord;

the Most High takes care of them.

16Therefore they will receive a glorious crown

and a beautiful diadem from the hand of the Lord,

because with his right hand he will cover them,

and with his arm he will shield them.

17The Lordc will take his zeal as his whole armor,

and will arm all creation to repeld his enemies;

18he will put on righteousness as a breastplate,

and wear impartial justice as a helmet;

19he will take holiness as an invincible shield,

20and sharpen stern wrath for a sword,

and creation will join with him to fight against his frenzied foes.

21Shafts of lightning will fly with true aim,

and will leap from the clouds to the target, as from a well-drawn bow,

22and hailstones full of wrath will be hurled as from a catapult;

the water of the sea will rage against them,

and rivers will relentlessly overwhelm them;

23a mighty wind will rise against them,

and like a tempest it will winnow them away.

Lawlessness will lay waste the whole earth,

and evildoing will overturn the thrones of rulers.

6

Listen therefore, O kings, and understand;

learn, O judges of the ends of the earth.

2Give ear, you that rule over multitudes,

and boast of many nations.

3For your dominion was given you from the Lord,

and your sovereignty from the Most High;

he will search out your works and inquire into your plans.

4Because as servants of his kingdom you did not rule rightly,

or keep the law,

or walk according to the purpose of God,

5he will come upon you terribly and swiftly,

because severe judgment falls on those in high places.

6For the lowliest may be pardoned in mercy,

but the mighty will be mightily tested.

7For the Lord of all will not stand in awe of anyone,

or show deference to greatness;

because he himself made both small and great,

and he takes thought for all alike.

8But a strict inquiry is in store for the mighty.

9To you then, O monarchs, my words are directed,

so that you may learn wisdom and not transgress.

10For they will be made holy who observe holy things in holiness,

and those who have been taught them will find a defense.

1.1–15: Exhortation to love righteousness. A series of oppositions form the basis of making a choice for justice. On one side are righteousness, the Lord, God, wisdom, a holy and disciplined spirit, a kindly spirit, and the spirit of the Lord (vv. 1–7). On the other side are perverse thoughts, a deceitful soul, foolish thoughts, unrighteousness, blasphemers, and death (vv. 3–13). These oppositions are a version of the doctrine of the two ways (e.g., Ps 1). 1: The book begins with the literary device of the apostrophe, a direct address to the hearers or readers, though the actual audience is the Hellenistic Jewish community, not their Gentile rulers. 7: Wisdom seems to be equated with the spirit of the Lord. 11: Tongue, the dangers of rash speech is a frequent topic in wisdom literature (e.g., Prov 2.12; 4.24; 10.11, 18–19, 31–32; Sir 9.18; 20.24–26; 28.12–26). 12: Death here is not simply the experience of mortality but rather a total annihilation and separation from God. 14: Hades, both the abode of the dead in Greek tradition, and the god who presides over the underworld.

Several events from Jacob's life are alluded to: flight from his brother Esau, Gen 27.41–45; Jacob's vision of the ladder at Bethel, the kingdom of God, Gen 28.10–17; Jacob's prosperity despite his oppressors, Laban and his sons, Gen 30.25–31.54; Jacob's wrestling with an angel in arduous contest at Peniel, Gen 32.22–32. 13–14: Joseph. Several events from Joseph's life are singled out from the lengthy narrative: being sold by his brothers, Gen 37.28; the unsuccessful seduction by Potiphar's wife (delivered from sin), Gen 39.6–20; being liberated from the prison, Gen 41.1–43; and Joseph's rise to power (scepter of a kingdom), Gen 41.37–57. 15–21: A holy people, Israel. A nation of oppressors, Egypt, when it oppressed the Israelites (Ex 1.8–14). The final contrast is more complex than the previous in that the righteous consists of the entire people of Israel and their singular leader, Moses. 16: Servant of the Lord, a frequent title for Moses (e.g., Ex 14.31; Num 12.7; Deut 34.5). 17: Shelter … starry flame, Ex 13.21–22. 18–19: The Red Sea, the only proper name mentioned in the book, Ex 14–15. 20: Plundered the ungodly, Ex 11.2; 12.35–36; sang hymns, Ex 15.1–21. 11.1: By the holy prophet, with this final reference to wisdom prospering the righteous through the hand of Moses, wisdom recedes into the background.
11.2–19.22: The Exodus and divine providence. Wisdom recedes into the background, while God is the protagonist who rewards the righteous and thwarts the godless. The proposition from the first part of the book, that God will “arm creation to repel his enemies” (5.17–23) is confirmed throughout this section. The section also exhibits the principle that the sins of the godless contain the seeds of their own punishment. Reflections on divine mercy and the source of idolatry are prominent themes. The entire reflection is ultimately folded into direct praise to God for the providential care of the just (10.20–21; 11.17–26; 19.22).
11.1–14: First contrast: plague of the Nile and water from the rock. 1: Holy prophet, Moses; cf. Deut 18:15; 34:10. 5–9: Through the very things, the inherent relationship between sin and punishment. The Egyptians drowned the Israelite infants in the Nile (11.7; cf. Ex 1.22) and were punished by the Nile becoming undrinkable (11.6; cf. Ex 7.14–24). In turn the Israelites received abundant water (Ex 17.1–7; Num 20.2–13). 8–10: The thirst of the righteous as divine pedagogy (cf. 3.3–7; Deut 8.2–5).
11.15–16.1–4: Second contrast: plague of irrational creatures versus the gift of quails. 11.15–16: Egyptian gods were often depicted with animal features; hence the plague of various animals (Ex 8.1–19; 9.1–7). Two theological reflections interrupt the contrast, which resumes in 16.1.
11.17–12.27: First theological reflection: the power and mercy of God. The reflection contrasts the manner in which God punishes: Egyptians are punished so that they repent (11.17–12.2), the Canaanites are punished moderately (12.3–18), the Israelites are punished mildly so that they learn to be merciful (12.19–22), and finally the Egyptians are punished so as to recognize the true God (12.23–27).
11.17–12.2: The moderation of God toward Egyptians. 17: Formless matter, a term borrowed from Greek philosophy (see Plato, Timaeus 50D-E); cf. the “formless void” of Gen 1.2). 20: Measure … number … weight, see Plato, Philebus 55E, Republic 602D. 12.1–2: Your immortal spirit is in all things, cf. 7.24–25; 15.11; Gen 2.7.
12.3–18: The moderation of God toward Canaanites, as an example of God's moderation toward sinners. 2: The purpose of punishment is rehabilitation; cf. 11.23. 4–8: Their detestable practices, see Lev 18.21–29; Deut 12.31; 18.10–12. 8: Wasps, see Ex 23.28; Deut 7.20; Josh 24.12. 13: Any god besides you, see Deut 32.39; Isa 44.6; cf. Ex 20.3.
12.19–22: Israel is to be merciful and to trust. 19: The manner of God's punishment of others and of Israel is a lesson to be emulated and grounds for hope.
12.23–27: The sins and punishments of Egypt. The very animals worshiped by the Egyptians became the instruments of the plagues; see 11.15–16n.; 15.18–19. 27: The true God, cf. Ex 12.31–32.
13.1–15.19: Second theological reflection: false worship. Three critiques: of philosophers who worship the natural world (13.1–9); of the worship of idols (13.10–15.13); of the enemies of Israel who worship both idols and odious animals (15.14–19).
13.1–9: The natural world. 1: Although Platonists considered the Supreme Being (the one who exists) as separate from the material creator, for Jews the Supreme Being is the same as the artisan of creation. 2–5: Fire, wind, air … luminaries … the elements of nature and the luminaries in the sky; see Deut 4.19; 17.3; 2 Kings 17.16; Job 31.26–28. 6–9: Worshipers of nature receive the least blame because they continue seeking God within the cosmos which is God's creation.
13.10–15.13: Polemic against idolatry. Idolatry is more blameworthy because idolaters worship human-made artifacts. For other critiques against idolatry see Pss 97.7; 115.3–8; 135.15–18; Isa 2.8–20; 40.18–20; 44.9–20; 45.16–20; 46.5–7; Jer 10.1–16; Hab 2.18; Bar 6.8–73. The criticism is simplistic, since no pagans regarded themselves as worshiping a piece of wood, stone, or metal. The satire is aimed at a Jewish audience and is similar to comments about idols in some Greco-Roman writers (Horace, Satires 1.8.1–7; Sophocles, according to Strom. 5.14.113.1–2).
13.10–19: The critique notes both the substance for the making of idols and the form into which they are made. 11: Cf. Isa 40.18–20; Jer 10.3–5. 17–19: Cf. Ps 115.3–8.
14.1–11: During travels on ship, safety comes from God and not from wooden idols. 2: Wisdom was the artisan, see 9.9–11. 3: Your providence, cf. 17.2; 4 Macc 9.24. O Father, cf. Isa 64.8. 6–7: Arrogant giants, see Gen 6.1–5; Sir 16.7; Bar 3.26–28. Took refuge on a raft, Noah's ark; blessed is the wood, referring to the ark. 11: Visitation, God's judgment upon the idols, cf. 3.7, Ex 12.12; Isa 2.18–20.
14.12–31: The origin and consequences of idolatry. The idea that human beings could be elevated to divine status occurs in Egypt and Mesopotamia and became common in the Greco-Roman world. 12: Fornication, a metaphor for worshiping other gods; see Jer 3.6–8; Ezek 16.15–43; Hos 4.11–19, cf. Ex 34.15–17. 15–21: Two examples provide the origins of idolatry: a statue in memory of a dead child (vv. 15–16) and of a king made as a representation for subjects in distant lands (vv. 17–21). 22–31: The consequences of idolatry are all sorts of vices. 23: Cf. 12.5; Lev 18.21. 25: See Hos 4.1–2. 31: The just penalty, cf. 11.16.
15.1–6: True worship of God brings righteousness and immortality. 1: The description of God's mercy alludes to Ex 34.6–7.
15.7–13: Clay idols. The critique is parallel to that of the carpenter (13.10–19). There is a progressive downgrade in the quality of all the substances used for idols: gold, silver, stone, wood, clay. 8: That souls were borrowed was a philosophical conception (cf. 15.16).
15.14–19: Enemies of Israel who worship idols and hateful animals. 14: Most foolish, the Egyptians who oppressed the Israelites (Ex 1.8–12). 18–19: Egyptian animal worship, see 11.15–16n. Romans, as well as Jews, criticized the practice, cf. Juvenal, Satire 15.1–11; Philo, Decalogue 76–80.
16.1–4: Second contrast resumes from 11.15–16. 1: Tormented, cf. 11.15 and Ex 8.1–15, the first plague of animals. Several of the plagues are combined for the second and third contrasts. 2–4: Instead … quails, the contrasting benefit to the righteous during the journey in the wilderness; cf. Num 11.31–34, where the Israelites’ demand for meat is criticized.
16.5–14. Third contrast: insects that kill versus the saving brazen serpent. 5: The third contrast uniquely begins with the suffering of the Israelites; see Num 21.5–6. 6: A symbol of deliverance, the serpent of bronze Moses was asked to make, Num 21.7–9. 9: In Ex 8.12–28 and 10.12–20 the Egyptians do not die from the insects, but Josephus (Ant 2.14.3) includes this detail. 12: Your word … heals all, the bronze serpent is not an idol, since it is God who heals, not the bronze object, cf. 16.7.
16.15–29: Fourth contrast: plague of storms versus the gift of manna. 16: The seventh plague was destruction by rains … hail … storms, and fire, Ex 9.22–26. 20: In contrast, the righteous received food of angels, the manna in the wilderness, see Ex 16.1–21; Num 11.6–9. The contrast emphasizes the forces of the universe which attack the ungodly but help the righteous, cf. 8.4–6; 16.17,24; 19.18–21. 21: To suit everyone's liking, a tradition reflected in b. Yoma 75a; Mekilta on Ex 16.23. 26: So that your children … might learn, cf. Deut 8.2–5.
17.1–18.4: Fifth contrast: plague of darkness versus a pillar of fire. The plague enslaves the unrighteous (Ex 10.21–23) because they had sinned by enslaving the Israelites. The description of terrors is reminiscent of the literary genre known as the “descent into Hades,” see Virgil, Aeneid 6. 17.7: The delusions of their magic art, see Ex 7.22; 8.7, 18–19; 9.11. 14: Hades, see 1.14n. 17.20–18.3: A brilliant light … a great light … a flaming pillar of fire, see Ex 13.21–22; cf. Num 9.15–16; Ps 78.14; 105.39. 18.4: The law was to be given to the world, see Isa 2.2–3; Mic 4.1–4; 2 Esd 7.20–24; 14.20–22.
18.5–25: Sixth contrast: destruction of children versus the blameless man who stops the destroyer. The death of the Egyptian firstborn is contrasted with the saving power of Aaron. 5: Wisdom connects Ex 1.15–22 with Ex 12.29–30; cf. Wis 11.6–14. One child, Moses. Flood, Ex 14.26–29. 9: The first Passover (Ex 12.1–28). 15: Your all-powerful word … a stern warrior, the destroyer in Ex 12.23 is the punishing side of God. If … leaped from heaven, from the royal throne is a reference to wisdom (cf. 9.10), this would be the only example of wisdom having a punishing role. 20: The experience of death by the righteous, cf. the punishment for rebellion in the wilderness in Num 16.23–35. 21–25: The blameless man, Aaron, who saves the Israelites in Num 16.41–50. 24: See Ex 28.15–28. On the symbolism of the high priest's vestments, see Josephus, Ant. 3.7.7; Philo, Moses 2.117–26.
19.1–12: Seventh contrast: the strange death versus the extraordinary deliverance at the Red Sea. The final contrast sets the drowning of Pharaoh's army against the deliverance of the Israelites (Ex 14.1–31). 3: Foolish decision, cf. Ex 14.5–9. 6–12: A recapitulation of several plagues and blessings highlighting the way the cosmos thwarts the unjust and helps the righteous. 6: The whole creation … fashioned anew, see 5.17–23; 16.24. 7: Cloud, Ex 13.21. 10–12: See 16.1–4; cf. Ex 8.1–19.
19.13–22. Final theological reflection: the debilitating consequences of injustice; the transforming power of the cosmos; concluding doxology praising God. 13–17: Hatred of strangers, a new argument is introduced to justify the punishments. Egypt had welcomed Joseph and the Israelites as guests whom they enslaved, see Gen 46–47; Ex 1; righteous man, Lot; see 10.6n. 18–21: The analogy of musical harmony is used to explain the transformative capacity of the cosmos; see Plato, Republic 7.12.530; Cicero, The Nature of the Gods 3.39.92. Three of the four elements of Greek philosophy are present—earth (land), water, and fire. Air is perhaps represented through music. 19: Land animals, water creatures, an unusual depiction of the Israelites passing through the sea, 20: Fire, cf. 16,17–23.
22:A final doxology praising God, reaches back to the righteous who sang hymns to God and praised God's defending hand, 10.20–21. The entire history of deliverance from Egypt is engulfed into a single voice of praise to God for thwarting injustice and helping righteousness.
